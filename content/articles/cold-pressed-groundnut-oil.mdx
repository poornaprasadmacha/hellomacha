  <h2 className="text-2xl md:text-3xl font-bold text-gray-900 mt-16 mb-6 tracking-tight !no-underline">
    What Exactly Are Cold Pressed Oils? (The Traditional Way)
  </h2>

  <p className="mb-6 text-lg text-gray-800 leading-relaxed">
    To truly appreciate the value of cold pressed groundnut oil, we must first understand how it is made. The term "cold-pressed" is not just a marketing buzzword; it is a literal description of a mechanical extraction process that has been used in India for thousands of years.
  </p>

  <p className="mb-6 text-lg text-gray-800 leading-relaxed">
    In a traditional <em>Kacchi Ghani</em> or <em>Mara Chekku</em> process, groundnuts are mechanically crushed at a relatively slow speed. Producers may use a wooden or metal press, and the details vary by mill. The term "cold pressed" generally describes extraction without the high-heat refining steps used in some other processes; it does not by itself certify a specific temperature, seed grade or laboratory result. Ask the maker how it defines the method if temperature control is important to you.
  </p>

  <p className="mb-6 text-lg text-gray-800 leading-relaxed">
    Processing conditions can affect an oil's flavour and some minor compounds, but it is too broad to say that heat makes all refined oils dangerous or that cold pressing preserves every nutrient unchanged. A useful comparison is practical: look at the ingredient declaration, extraction method, best-before date and storage advice, and choose a flavour and cooking use that suit your kitchen.
  </p>

  <p className="mb-6 text-lg text-gray-800 leading-relaxed">
    The resulting oil may have a deeper colour and a noticeable groundnut aroma compared with a highly refined product. Colour and smell can help you recognise a style you enjoy, but they are not reliable tests of purity on their own.
  </p>

  <h2 className="text-2xl md:text-3xl font-bold text-gray-900 mt-16 mb-6 tracking-tight !no-underline">
    What Are Refined Oils & Why Are They Dangerous?
  </h2>

  <p className="mb-6 text-lg text-gray-800 leading-relaxed">
    Refined oils are designed for consistent flavour, appearance and shelf life, and the refining process can include several stages such as degumming, neutralisation, bleaching and deodorisation. Extraction and refining methods vary by oil and producer; they are not proof of adulteration. The following list describes common processing stages, not a claim that every brand uses identical chemicals, temperatures or equipment.
  </p>

  <p className="mb-6 text-lg text-gray-800 leading-relaxed">
    To understand why you should immediately remove refined oils from your kitchen, you need to look at the brutal chemical and high-temperature processing they undergo:
  </p>

  <ul className="list-disc list-inside space-y-3 mb-8 text-lg text-gray-800">
    <li><strong>Chemical Solvent Extraction:</strong> After the initial mechanical crush, the leftover seed pulp is washed with a petroleum derivative called <em>Hexane</em>. This highly toxic solvent dissolves the remaining oil from the seed. While manufacturers claim the Hexane is evaporated later, trace amounts can remain.</li>
    <li><strong>Degumming with Acid:</strong> The extracted crude oil is then treated with phosphoric acid to remove naturally occurring gums and phospholipids, which are actually beneficial for human digestion but reduce the oil's shelf life.</li>
    <li><strong>Chemical Neutralization:</strong> Sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) is added to the oil to neutralize free fatty acids. This process creates a soap-like byproduct that must be washed out with water.</li>
    <li><strong>Bleaching:</strong> The oil is passed through bleaching clays to strip away its natural, beautiful color, turning it into the pale, clear liquid you see in plastic pouches at the supermarket.</li>
    <li><strong>High-Heat Deodorization:</strong> Finally, the oil is heated to extreme temperatures (often reaching 230°C–260°C) to remove its natural smell. This extreme heat forces the creation of dangerous <em>trans fats</em> and <em>glycidyl esters</em>, compounds heavily linked to cardiovascular diseases, arterial plaque buildup, and chronic inflammation.</li>
  </ul>

  <p className="mb-6 text-lg text-gray-800 leading-relaxed">
    Refining can reduce some naturally occurring minor compounds, while the fatty-acid profile depends mainly on the source oil. The amount of any nutrient retained varies by product and process, so a universal percentage loss is not a safe basis for comparing bottles. Refined oil still provides energy and fat; it should not be described as "empty" or inherently inflammatory.
  </p>

  <h2 className="text-2xl md:text-3xl font-bold text-gray-900 mt-16 mb-6 tracking-tight !no-underline">
    Health Benefits of Cold Pressed Groundnut Oil
  </h2>

  <p className="mb-6 text-lg text-gray-800 leading-relaxed">
    Cold-pressed groundnut oil can be chosen for its familiar flavour and cooking characteristics, but it is still an energy-dense fat. It should not be presented as a treatment for heart disease, diabetes or digestive problems. If a clinician has advised you to limit a particular fat or you have a peanut allergy, follow that advice and check product handling and cross-contact information.
  </p>

  <ul className="list-disc list-inside space-y-3 mb-8 text-lg text-gray-800">
    <li><strong>Fat composition:</strong> Groundnut oil contains monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats. Its overall contribution to a diet depends on the amounts used and the rest of the foods eaten; no single cooking oil can by itself guarantee a particular cholesterol result.</li>
    <li><strong>Cooking temperature:</strong> Groundnut oil is used in many Indian recipes, including sautéing and frying. The smoke point varies with processing, quality and age of the oil, so follow the product's directions, avoid overheating and do not treat a smoke point as a guarantee that repeated deep-frying is harmless.</li>
    <li><strong>Minor nutrients:</strong> Less-refined oils may retain some naturally occurring compounds, including vitamin E, but levels vary between products. An oil's presence of antioxidants does not establish a treatment effect or prevent skin ageing or illness.</li>
    <li><strong>Medical needs:</strong> Research on individual fatty acids does not make groundnut oil a recommended treatment for diabetes or a way to improve insulin production. People managing diabetes or another condition should follow their clinician's dietary advice.</li>
    <li><strong>Digestion and allergies:</strong> Cold-pressed describes a process, not a guarantee about digestion, gut health or the absence of all residues. Groundnut is a peanut product; people with a peanut allergy should avoid it unless their allergy specialist advises otherwise.</li>
  </ul>
