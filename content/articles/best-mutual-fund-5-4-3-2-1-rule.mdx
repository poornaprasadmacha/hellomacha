  <h2 className="text-3xl md:text-4xl font-black text-gray-900 mt-16 mb-8 tracking-tight">
    How the 5-4-3-2-1 Rule Helps Find a Better Mutual Fund
  </h2>

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700">
    The 5-4-3-2-1 rule is a simple way to filter mutual funds using five basic checks. Instead of asking, <strong>"Which mutual fund gave the highest return?"</strong>, ask:
  </p>

  <ol className="list-decimal list-inside space-y-2 mb-8 text-gray-700 font-medium pl-2">
    <li>Does the fund have enough history?</li>
    <li>Has it performed well against its benchmark?</li>
    <li>Has it shown consistency?</li>
    <li>Am I buying too many similar funds?</li>
    <li>Does the fund have stable management?</li>
  </ol>

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700">
    This approach can be useful for Indian investors building investments for goals such as retirement, children's education, a home, or long-term wealth creation.
  </p>

  <blockquote className="text-xl md:text-2xl font-serif italic text-gray-900 border-l-2 border-gray-900 pl-6 my-12">
    However, no checklist can identify the best mutual fund with certainty. Mutual fund investments are subject to market risk, and past performance does not guarantee future performance. SEBI's investor guidance specifically warns investors not to treat past performance as a guarantee of future returns.
  </blockquote>

  {/* SECTION 2 */}
  <h2 className="text-3xl font-black text-gray-900 mt-16 mb-6 tracking-tight">
    5: Look for 5+ Years of History
  </h2>

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700 text-lg">
    The first filter is simple: <strong>look for funds with at least five years of history when your goal is to evaluate long-term consistency.</strong>
  </p>

  <img 
    src="https://hellomacha.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/5-Year-Mutual-Fund-History-1024x683.webp" 
    alt="Five years of mutual fund history for choosing a better fund" 
    className="w-full h-auto object-cover border border-gray-100 my-10"
  />

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700">
    Why five years? A longer history gives you more information about how a fund behaved across different market conditions. You can study its performance during strong markets as well as periods of weakness. For example, a fund that looks excellent after one strong year may not have the same record over a longer period.
  </p>

  <p className="mb-4 font-bold text-gray-900">A five-year history can help you investigate:</p>
  <ul className="list-disc list-inside space-y-2 mb-8 text-gray-700">
    <li>Performance across different market conditions</li>
    <li>Returns over multiple periods</li>
    <li>Changes in the portfolio</li>
    <li>Changes in fund management</li>
    <li>Consistency against the benchmark</li>
    <li>Risk and volatility</li>
  </ul>

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700">
    However, <strong>older does not automatically mean better</strong>. A fund with a long history can still be unsuitable for your goals or risk level. Use five years as a screening point, not as the final decision.
  </p>
  
  <p className="text-sm font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-red-700 mb-16">

  {/* SECTION 3 */}
  <h2 className="text-3xl font-black text-gray-900 mt-16 mb-6 tracking-tight">
    4: Check Performance Against the Benchmark
  </h2>

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700 text-lg">
    The second number in the rule is <strong>4</strong>. The idea is to check whether the fund has beaten its relevant benchmark in <strong>at least four of the last five years</strong>, rather than celebrating one exceptional year.
  </p>

  <img 
    src="https://hellomacha.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Mutual-Fund-Performance-vs-Benchmark-1024x683.webp"

  />

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700">
    A benchmark gives you a reference point for evaluating performance. SEBI has required mutual fund performance comparisons with benchmarks, and scheme documents disclose the applicable benchmark for evaluating performance.
  </p>

Benchmark comparison only makes sense when you compare the fund with an appropriate benchmark. Also remember that <strong>beating a benchmark once does not prove that a fund will continue to outperform it</strong>.

  {/* SECTION 4 */}
  <h2 className="text-3xl font-black text-gray-900 mt-16 mb-6 tracking-tight">
    3: Look for Top-Quartile Consistency
  </h2>

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700 text-lg">
    The third number is <strong>3</strong>. The framework suggests looking for funds that have ranked in the <strong>top 25% of their category at least three times</strong> during the period you are studying.
  </p>

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700">
    Suppose Fund A gives 35% in one year but performs poorly in several other years. Fund B gives more moderate returns but remains competitive over multiple periods. Simply chasing Fund A because of one spectacular year may not be sensible.
  </p>

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700">
    The goal is not to find a fund that wins every year. That is unrealistic. The goal is to understand whether the fund has shown <strong>reasonable consistency over time</strong>.
  </p>

  {/* SECTION 5 */}
  <h2 className="text-3xl font-black text-gray-900 mt-16 mb-6 tracking-tight">
    2: Keep Funds Limited Within a Category
  </h2>

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700 text-lg">
    The fourth part is <strong>2</strong>. The idea is to avoid collecting too many mutual funds that invest in similar areas. Having 10 or 15 mutual funds does not automatically mean you are well diversified.
  </p>

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700">
    For example, suppose you own four large-cap funds. If their portfolios contain many of the same companies, you may have more <strong>portfolio overlap</strong> than you realise. That can make your portfolio complicated without adding much diversification.
  </p>

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700 font-medium">
    Before adding another fund, ask: "What does this fund add to my existing portfolio?"
  </p>
  
  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700">
    The exact number of funds suitable for you depends on your portfolio, goals, risk tolerance and investment strategy. So <strong>2 should be treated as a simple screening idea, not a universal rule for every investor.</strong>
  </p>

  <h2 className="text-3xl font-black text-gray-900 mt-16 mb-6 tracking-tight">
    1: Check Fund Manager Stability
  </h2>

  <p className="mb-6 text-gray-700 text-lg">
    The final number is <strong>1</strong>. The framework suggests checking whether the fund has a relatively stable fund manager with meaningful experience, such as three or more years managing the relevant strategy.
  </p>

But don't choose a fund <strong>only because of one manager's name</strong>. Fund houses can change managers, teams and strategies. Therefore, the manager should be one part of your research rather than the deciding factor.
  </p>
